# How Much Does a Salon Website Cost in 2026?

A salon website should do three jobs well. It needs to book appointments, show real work, and answer the questions people ask before they call.

If it does none of that, it is just expensive wallpaper. Most salons do not need a giant website. They need one that makes booking easier and trust faster.

## What a salon website actually has to do

A good salon site is not just about looking pretty. It has to help someone decide, book, and show up.

At minimum, it should explain services in plain English, list hours and location, show real photos, and make contact easy. For a hair salon, nail salon, spa, or barbershop, that is the job.

### The basics that matter
- Services with short, clear descriptions
- Location, hours, and parking details
- Click-to-call buttons on mobile
- Booking or request-an-appointment flow
- Stylist bios and photos
- Reviews or testimonials
- A simple FAQ about pricing, deposits, and cancellations

People compare salons on their phone while sitting in a car, or while their color is processing somewhere else. They do not want a mystery novel. They want to know who you are, what you do, and how fast they can book.

## How much a salon website costs in 2026

Here is the honest range.

| Build type | Typical cost | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starter site | $2,000 to $5,000 | New salons, solo stylists, simple brochure sites |
| Growth site | $5,000 to $12,000 | Salons that want more bookings and local search traffic |
| Advanced site | $12,000 to $25,000+ | Multi-location brands, memberships, custom booking flows |

A starter site is usually a clean custom design or a solid template, a handful of pages, and basic contact forms. It gets the business online without making the owner learn web design.

A growth site adds more service pages, better copy, better photos, local SEO work, and stronger booking setup. This is where a salon starts getting more than just an online business card.

An advanced site is for salons with several locations, stylists with different schedules, memberships, gift cards, waitlists, or custom integrations. That extra complexity costs real money, because every extra moving part needs setup and testing.

### Ongoing costs
Plan on ongoing costs too. Hosting, maintenance, updates, and backups often run $75 to $400 per month for a small salon. If the site uses a booking platform, CRM, or email tool, that can add another monthly bill.

That monthly number is normal. Cheap sites get expensive when nobody maintains them.

## What changes the price the most

Two salon websites can look similar and still cost very different amounts. Scope is the reason.

### Page count
A five-page site costs less than a twenty-page site. Every service page, stylist page, and city page takes planning, writing, design, and testing.

Salons often need more pages than they think. A generic services page is weak. Separate pages for balayage, color correction, extensions, facial treatments, or bridal styling can do much better in search.

### Booking setup
A simple contact form is cheap. Real booking logic is not.

If the salon wants online booking, deposits, reminders, cancellations, or routing by stylist, the build gets more complex. If the booking tool has to connect with calendars or a CRM, the setup takes longer.

### Photos and branding
Good salon photography matters more than people want to admit. Stock photos of smiling models with perfect teeth do not help. Real photos of the space, staff, and work build trust.

If the salon needs a logo refresh, brand colors, custom photography, or copywriting, expect the budget to rise. That work pays off, because salons sell trust as much as they sell service.

### SEO and local pages
Salons compete locally, so location matters. A salon in Shadyside is not fighting the same search results as a shop in a smaller town.

Local SEO work can include service pages, location pages, title tags, schema markup, and internal links. That is not fluff. It helps the site show up when people search for “hair salon near me” or “balayage in Pittsburgh.”

### Multi-location and memberships
A single-location salon is easy. A brand with several locations, different hours, different stylists, and different offers is a bigger job.

Membership plans, gift cards, referral programs, and loyalty tools add more setup. They also add more chances for something to break later, which is why the price keeps climbing.

## Template vs custom: which one wins?

A template site is fine for a brand-new salon or a small shop that needs to get online fast. It is cheaper, quicker, and usually good enough if the business is simple.

Custom wins when the salon wants to stand out, rank in a crowded market, or make booking feel polished. That matters in competitive neighborhoods and busy cities, especially when every nearby salon has a half-baked Instagram bio and a link tree.

The rule is simple. If the salon is basic, a template can work. If the salon needs more bookings, better search visibility, or a stronger brand, custom usually pays for itself.

## Features that actually bring in clients

Some features are nice. Others bring in money.

### Click-to-call and sticky booking buttons
Most salon visitors are on phones. A tap-to-call button removes friction.

Sticky booking buttons help too. People should not hunt for the next step while deciding on a color appointment.

### Clear service menus
A salon website should explain services without sounding like a price sheet from 2009. Short descriptions help people understand the difference between services and pick the right one.

If exact pricing changes often, list “starting at” prices. That is better than hiding everything.

### Stylist pages
Stylist pages build trust and help people book the right person. Include names, specialties, short bios, and a few real photos.

This is especially useful for salons where clients care about the person behind the chair, which is most of them.

### Before and after gallery
A gallery matters because hair, nails, skin, and spa services are visual. Real work sells faster than adjectives.

Keep the gallery fast and organized. Nobody wants to wait for a giant image slider to load on mobile.

### Reviews and FAQ
Reviews help close the deal. A short FAQ reduces calls about parking, deposits, no-shows, cancellation rules, and who does what.

That saves time for the front desk and helps people book with fewer doubts.

## When to rebuild instead of patching

Sometimes the problem is not one missing feature. It is the whole site.

A rebuild makes sense when the current site is slow on phones, hard to edit, broken on booking, or stuck in an old design. It also makes sense when the salon has grown past the original website.

Look for these warning signs:
- The phone number is hard to find
- Forms do not work
- Pages load slowly on mobile
- The design looks old enough to rent a video store
- SEO changes are hard to make
- Staff cannot update hours or promos without help

If three or more of those are true, patching is usually a bad deal. A rebuild costs more up front, but endless fixes cost more in the long run.

## The bottom line

For most salons, the sweet spot is a $5,000 to $12,000 website. That range usually covers good design, booking support, local SEO, and the kind of content that actually helps people book.

A smaller salon can spend less. A multi-location brand or a salon with memberships and custom tools can spend a lot more. The right budget is the one that fits the business model, not the one that sounds cheap in a sales call.

## FAQ

### How much does a small salon website cost in 2026?
A small salon website usually costs $2,000 to $5,000 if it is mostly informational. If you want stronger SEO, better photos, and booking support, the budget moves closer to $5,000 to $12,000.

### Do salons need online booking?
Yes, in most cases. Online booking cuts phone tag and helps people act while they are already interested. It also makes the site more useful on mobile.

### Is Squarespace good for a salon website?
Squarespace can work for a simple salon site. It is fine for basic pages and a clean design, but it can feel limiting once you need deeper SEO, custom booking logic, or multi-location setup.

### What should a hair salon website include?
It should include services, prices or starting prices, stylist bios, photos, booking, hours, location, and reviews. A short FAQ and contact options are also smart.

### How long does it take to build a salon website?
A simple salon site can take 2 to 4 weeks. A custom site with better copy, photo work, and booking setup usually takes 4 to 8 weeks.
