# How Much Does a Real Estate Website Cost in 2026?

A real estate website is not just a digital business card. It has to earn trust, show listings, and turn visitors into leads without making the user do homework.

That is why the price range is wider than most people expect. A solo agent site and a brokerage site with IDX, neighborhood pages, and lead routing are not the same animal.

## What a real estate website actually does

A good real estate site has three jobs. It needs to make the business look credible, help people find homes, and capture leads before they wander off to Zillow again.

### It sells trust first

Buyers and sellers judge fast. They want to know who you are, what areas you cover, and whether you look like you can actually close a deal.

That means clean design, strong agent bios, reviews, and a contact form that works on mobile. Fancy animations do not matter if the phone number is hard to find.

### IDX is useful, but not always required

IDX is the property search system that pulls listings from the MLS onto your site. It is handy for teams and brokerages, and it can help keep people on your site longer.

But a new solo agent does not always need full IDX on day one. Sometimes a strong local site with landing pages, seller lead forms, and a few smart integrations is the better move.

## Real estate website cost ranges in 2026

Here is the honest breakdown. These numbers assume a professional build, not a cousin who “knows websites.” That cousin usually disappears right after launch.

### Starter site: $1,500 to $4,000

This is the budget range for a solo agent or a new team with basic needs. It usually includes a homepage, about page, contact page, service areas, and a simple lead form.

A site in this range often uses a template or a light custom setup. It works well if the goal is to look professional, show up in local search, and collect calls.

### Growth site: $4,000 to $12,000

This is the sweet spot for most serious agents and small teams. You get more custom design, better copy, neighborhood pages, stronger SEO setup, and cleaner lead capture.

At this level, the site can support buyer and seller funnels, content marketing, review embeds, and CRM connections. It feels less like a brochure and more like a sales tool.

### Brokerage or IDX-heavy site: $12,000 to $30,000+

This range fits brokerages, multi-agent teams, and businesses that need search, filtering, agent directories, and more complex integrations. IDX work adds time, and MLS rules are not known for being charming.

If the site also needs custom search behavior, saved favorites, advanced forms, or multi-location pages, the price climbs fast. That is normal. The site is doing real work now.

## What changes the price the most

Two real estate sites can look similar and cost very different amounts. Scope is the whole story.

### IDX and MLS integration

This is the biggest swing factor. Some MLS feeds are simple. Others have setup steps, approval rules, and display limits that eat time.

If the site needs listing search, map filters, neighborhood boundaries, and listing detail pages, expect the price to move up. More moving parts means more build time and more testing.

### Number of pages

A five-page site is cheaper than a thirty-page site. That part is not a mystery.

But real estate sites often need separate pages for buyers, sellers, neighborhoods, communities, property types, and agent profiles. In Pittsburgh, one page for everything from Shadyside to Cranberry is not enough.

### Copy, photography, and video

Good copy is not filler. It explains what makes the agent or brokerage different, and it gives search engines more to work with.

Professional photos and video also matter. Real estate is visual. Grainy headshots and phone photos send the wrong message fast.

### CRM and lead routing

If a form submission needs to go to a CRM, an email sequence, and the right agent, the build gets more technical. That kind of plumbing takes planning.

It also saves time later. A lead that lands in the right place gets answered faster, and fast answers win deals.

## Template vs custom: which one wins?

The right answer depends on where the business is today. Not every agent needs a custom build, and not every team should settle for a template that looks like every other listing site on the block.

### Template wins for solo agents on a budget

A template site is the best fit when the goal is speed and lower cost. It gets the basics done, keeps the budget sane, and gives you a professional presence online.

That said, templates can look generic. If your competition all bought the same theme, your brand may end up looking like a photocopy of a photocopy.

### Custom wins for teams and brokerages

Custom design makes sense when the site needs to do more than sit there and look polite. Teams with active lead gen, multiple agents, neighborhoods pages, and IDX search usually need a custom setup.

Custom also wins when local SEO matters. You can build pages that target specific suburbs, condo buildings, or buyer intent without stuffing everything into one sad page.

## Ongoing costs you should budget for

A website is not a one-time bill. It needs hosting, updates, backups, and a little attention so it does not rot quietly in the corner.

### Common monthly costs

Most real estate websites have some mix of these costs:

- Hosting: $25 to $100 per month
- Maintenance and updates: $50 to $300 per month
- IDX or MLS plugins: $30 to $150 per month
- CRM tools: varies a lot
- Email marketing or automation tools: varies a lot

The exact total depends on the stack. A site with five tools glued together costs more to run than a simple one with fewer pieces.

### What people forget

The hidden cost is usually content. Neighborhood pages, listing pages, blog posts, and seasonal updates all take time.

A site can launch for a fair price and still fail if nobody updates it. Search engines love fresh, useful pages. They are less impressed by a site that has not changed since the Penguins were still terrifying people.

## What a good real estate website should include before launch

Before the site goes live, make sure the basics are in place. This is the part that keeps the site from looking nice but performing like a brick.

### The core checklist

A solid real estate website should have:

- A clear homepage with a strong headline
- Agent or brokerage bio pages
- Buyer and seller pages
- Service area or neighborhood pages
- A contact form that works on mobile
- Calls to action on every major page
- Reviews or testimonials
- Fast load times
- Proper SEO titles and meta descriptions
- Analytics and conversion tracking

If the business uses IDX, test the search flow on mobile. Many people browse listings on their phone while pretending to listen in a meeting.

### Good local SEO makes the site pay for itself

The site should target the places people actually search for. That might be city names, neighborhoods, school districts, or property types.

A Pittsburgh brokerage could create pages for Lawrenceville, Mount Lebanon, Squirrel Hill, and other areas it truly serves. That is better than one giant page that says nothing useful.

## Bottom line

A real estate website in 2026 usually costs $1,500 to $4,000 for a basic agent site, $4,000 to $12,000 for a serious lead-gen site, and $12,000 to $30,000+ for brokerage or IDX-heavy builds.

The cheapest option is not always the best deal. A site that answers buyer questions, supports sellers, and routes leads cleanly is the one that earns its keep.
