# How Much Does a Law Firm Website Cost in 2026?

A law firm website has one job, turn a search into a consultation. Pretty design helps, but clear practice pages, trust signals, and a smooth intake path matter more.

Most firms do not need a giant build on day one. They need a site that answers questions quickly, looks credible on mobile, and makes calling or submitting a form painless.

## What a law firm website actually has to do

A legal website is not a digital brochure. It is the front desk, the intake line, and the first sales call rolled into one.

That means the site should do a few practical things fast:
- Say what the firm handles
- Show where it works
- Introduce the attorneys
- Make contact easy
- Build enough trust to get a call

### Practice area pages matter more than fancy homepage sliders

People rarely search for a law firm in general. They search for a problem, then they want a lawyer who handles that problem.

That is why practice area pages matter so much. A family law page, a personal injury page, and an estate planning page do very different jobs. One generic services page usually underperforms all three.

### Trust signals are not optional

People hiring a lawyer are usually stressed, confused, or both. They want to know who they are dealing with before they call.

Good trust signals include attorney bios, bar admissions, years in practice, office photos, review snippets, and clear contact information. Keep the tone calm and direct. Nobody wants copy that sounds like it was written during a closing argument.

## How much a law firm website costs in 2026

Here is the honest range.

| Build type | Typical cost | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starter site | $2,500 to $6,000 | Solo attorneys and small firms that need a credible web presence |
| Growth site | $6,000 to $15,000 | Firms that want better SEO, more practice pages, and stronger lead flow |
| Advanced site | $15,000 to $35,000+ | Competitive firms, multi-location practices, and custom intake workflows |

A starter site covers the basics. Think clean design, a handful of pages, mobile-friendly layout, and contact forms that work.

A growth site adds better writing, more practice pages, local SEO, and a structure that can actually rank. That is usually the sweet spot for most firms.

An advanced site is for firms with more moving parts. Maybe there are multiple attorneys, several offices, intake routing, or content built for aggressive search growth.

### Ongoing costs are part of the bill

A website is not a one-and-done purchase. Hosting, backups, security, updates, and small edits usually run $75 to $400 per month for a small firm.

If the site also gets monthly SEO work, content updates, or analytics reporting, the budget goes higher. That is normal. A website that never gets touched tends to age like milk.

## What changes the price the most

Two law firm websites can look similar and still cost very different amounts. Scope is usually the reason.

### Number of practice areas and pages

More pages mean more planning, writing, design, and testing. A five-page site is cheaper than a twenty-page site. That part is not mysterious.

Law firms also tend to need more content than they expect. A generic services page is weak. Specific practice pages, city pages, and FAQ pages give search engines more to work with and give visitors better answers.

### Copywriting and photography

Good copy is not decoration. It tells a nervous visitor what the firm handles, who it helps, and why it should be trusted.

Professional photography matters too. Stock photos of people in spotless suits are easy to spot and even easier to ignore. Real photos of the team, the office, and the building make the firm feel real.

### Intake forms, scheduling, and CRM connections

A basic contact form is cheap. A form that sends leads into a CRM, triggers follow-up text messages, and routes inquiries to the right person takes more work.

That extra setup can save hours later. It also keeps leads from sitting in an inbox for two days while someone is in court or out to lunch.

### Local SEO and structured data

A firm in downtown Pittsburgh faces different competition than a solo office in a quieter market. If the site needs to win local search, the build has to support that goal.

That usually means location pages, proper titles and descriptions, schema markup, map embeds, internal links, and pages written around real search terms. Search engines still need plain language. They are smart, not psychic.

### Accessibility and compliance

Legal websites should be easy to use on a phone, keyboard-friendly, and readable by people who are not in perfect lighting. That is good business and good risk management.

The harder a site is to use, the more expensive it becomes to fix. Patch jobs cost more than doing the structure right the first time.

## Template vs custom: which one wins

A template is fine when a firm is small, simple, and just needs to get online. It is faster, cheaper, and usually good enough for a solo attorney or a new practice.

Custom design wins when the firm has real competition. That includes personal injury, family law, criminal defense, and any market where everyone else also claims to be trusted, experienced, and aggressive.

### When a template makes sense

A template makes sense if the firm needs:
- A clean homepage
- A few core practice pages
- Basic contact and intake forms
- Simple branding
- A fast launch

In that case, the budget can stay lean without turning the site into a mess.

### When custom is the better bet

Custom is the better bet when the firm needs:
- A stronger brand
- Better conversion paths
- More service pages
- Better search visibility
- Integrations with intake or CRM tools

A downtown Pittsburgh firm trying to stand out in a crowded practice area is not in the same lane as a small estate planning office. The site should match the job.

## Features that actually bring in consultations

The best law firm websites are not the prettiest ones. They are the ones that make people act.

### Click-to-call and sticky mobile buttons

Most legal searches happen on phones. People do not want to hunt for a phone number like it is buried treasure.

A click-to-call button in the header and a sticky call button on mobile can make a real difference. That is one of the cheapest features with the biggest payoff.

### Plain-English practice pages

Each major practice area should get its own page. The page should explain what the firm handles, who it helps, and what the next step looks like.

Avoid legal jargon unless it is needed. The average visitor does not want a lecture. They want to know whether the firm can help and how fast they can talk to someone.

### FAQs that answer real questions

FAQ sections help with both conversions and search. They also save office staff from answering the same five questions every day.

Good law firm FAQ topics include cost, consultation length, what to bring, and whether the firm handles urgent matters. Answer them directly. No fluff.

### Reviews, schema, and location details

Reviews build trust fast. Schema helps search engines understand the business and the pages.

Add office hours, service areas, map embeds, and clear location details. If the firm serves multiple cities, build pages for them with actual useful content, not just a list of city names pasted into the footer.

## When to rebuild instead of patching

Some websites can be repaired. Others are just expensive habits.

A rebuild makes sense when the site is slow, hard to edit, weak on mobile, or missing basic lead tracking. It also makes sense when the design looks dated enough that visitors assume the firm is not keeping up.

Here is a simple test. If three or more of these are true, a rebuild is probably cheaper than endless fixes:
- The phone number is hard to find
- Forms break or go nowhere
- Mobile pages feel clumsy
- Small edits take too long
- Search traffic is flat
- The site looks older than the firm wants

A fresh build gives the firm a clean structure, better content, and less technical debt. That is usually a better use of money than paying someone to keep a tired site alive with duct tape and optimism.

## FAQ

### How much does a small law firm website cost?

A small law firm website usually costs $2,500 to $6,000 for a solid starter build. If the firm wants stronger local SEO, practice pages, and better copy, budget $6,000 to $15,000.

### Do law firms need local SEO on their website?

Yes. Most legal searches are local, even when the query sounds broad. A good site should target city and practice area pages, use schema, and make the office easy to find.

### Should an attorney website use a template or custom design?

A template is fine for a new solo practice with simple needs. Custom design makes more sense when the firm competes in a crowded market or needs stronger branding and lead tracking.

### What pages should a law firm website have?

At minimum, it should have a home page, practice area pages, attorney bios, contact information, and location or service area pages. FAQs and intake details help too.

### How long does it take to build a law firm website?

A simple build can take 2 to 4 weeks. A custom site with copywriting, SEO, and integrations usually takes 4 to 8 weeks.

## Bottom line

A law firm website should do one thing well, turn attention into consultations. If it does not help people call, book, or send a form, it is just expensive decoration.

For most small firms, a realistic budget is $2,500 to $6,000 for a basic site and $6,000 to $15,000 for a site built to grow. Bigger firms and more competitive markets can run much higher.
