# How Much Does a Landscaping Website Cost in 2026?

A landscaping website has one job: turn a seasonal search into a phone call or quote request. It does not need to win a design trophy from someone with a Figma account and too much coffee.

That is why pricing is messy. A small site can be inexpensive. A site that brings in jobs needs better copy, better photos, and a layout that makes it easy to call.

## What a landscaping website actually has to do

A good landscaping site answers the same questions a receptionist answers all day.

What services do you offer? What areas do you serve? Do you handle mowing, planting, hardscaping, irrigation, mulch, or snow removal? Can I get a quote without playing phone tag?

For most landscaping companies, the website also needs to work by season. In Pittsburgh, spring cleanup, mulch installation, patio work, leaf removal, and winter service all pull different buyers. A site that treats every service like the same job leaves money on the table.

The site also has to build trust fast. Homeowners want to see real crew photos, real trucks, real work, and a business that looks organized. No one wants to hire a company that looks like it was assembled during a lunch break.

## Landscaping website cost ranges in 2026

Here is the honest range.

| Build type | Typical cost | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starter site | $1,500 to $4,000 | Solo landscapers, small crews, or new companies that need a solid presence |
| Growth site | $4,000 to $10,000 | Companies that want better leads, local SEO, and more service pages |
| Advanced site | $10,000 to $25,000+ | Multi-location companies, booking workflows, CRM integrations, or custom lead capture |
| Ongoing maintenance | $50 to $300 per month | Hosting, updates, backups, security, and small content changes |

A starter site usually uses a strong template, custom branding, a handful of pages, and basic contact forms. It is enough to look professional and start getting found.

A growth site adds more service pages, better writing, local SEO setup, service-area pages, and a layout built around quotes.

An advanced site is for companies that need more moving parts. That might include online booking, quote routing, financing, image uploads, CRM links, seasonal landing pages, or separate pages for multiple locations.

## What changes the price the most

Two landscaping websites can look similar and still cost very different amounts. Scope is the reason.

### Number of services and pages

A company that only mows lawns needs less than one that handles design, installation, maintenance, lighting, drainage, and snow removal.

Each service should usually get its own page. Search engines understand that better, and homeowners do too. A generic services page is fine for a very small shop. It gets weak fast once the company grows.

### Copy and photos

Good copy is not filler. It explains what you do, who you help, and why someone should call you instead of the other crew down the road.

Real photos matter even more. Stock photos of perfect grass and fake sunsets are obvious. If the site has crews, equipment, and finished jobs, show them. That builds trust faster than a paragraph about excellence.

### Local SEO and service areas

If the company serves Pittsburgh, Mt. Lebanon, Bethel Park, Cranberry, Wexford, or nearby suburbs, the site should say so in a clean way. Service-area pages help the site show up for local searches.

This is where the work starts to matter. A page for "landscaping in Pittsburgh" is not the same as a page for "hardscaping in Cranberry." The intent is different, so the content should be different too.

### Forms, scheduling, and quote workflows

A basic contact form is cheap. A good quote workflow is not.

If the site needs image uploads, appointment scheduling, estimate routing, SMS alerts, or CRM integration, the price climbs. That is normal. Those features save time later, but they take setup time now.

### Hosting, maintenance, and updates

A website is not a one-time bill. It needs hosting, backups, plugin updates, security checks, and occasional content changes.

If the company runs seasonal promotions, winter services, or spring cleanup campaigns, someone has to update the site. If nobody owns it, the website slowly turns into digital yard waste.

## What landscaping companies waste money on

A lot of landscaping sites spend money in the wrong places.

### Giant homepage videos

A video of a mower cutting grass can look nice. It also slows the site down if nobody compresses it properly.

Speed matters. Homeowners on mobile do not wait around for a cinematic blade-of-grass moment. They tap the back button and call the company that loaded first.

### Fancy sliders

Sliders are old news. They usually hide the useful stuff, like the phone number and service area.

One strong headline and one clear call to action beat a carousel every time. Carousels are what people add when they do not know what to say.

### One page trying to do everything

A single page can work for a tiny business. It usually fails for a company with multiple services.

Mowing, mulch, hardscaping, drainage, and snow removal are different jobs. They need different messaging. Put them on one page and the site starts sounding like a bargain bin with a logo.

### Too much polish, not enough proof

A slick design with no reviews, no before-and-after photos, and no clear service area does not convert well.

Customers want proof. They want to know the company shows up, finishes the job, and answers the phone. That matters more than a fancy font pairing.

## What a good landscaping website should include before launch

Before a site goes live, it should pass a practical checklist.

### Core pages

At minimum, the site should have:

- Home
- About
- Services
- Service area pages
- Gallery or project photos
- Contact
- FAQ

If the company offers higher-value work like patios, retaining walls, or drainage, those should get their own pages too.

### Strong conversion details

The phone number should be in the header. The contact form should be easy to find. Mobile buttons should make calling simple.

A quote form should ask for the service needed, the property address or neighborhood, and room for photos. The more context the business gets up front, the less back-and-forth it needs later.

### Trust signals

Reviews help. Insurance info helps. Years in business help. Crew photos help.

If the company serves a few neighborhoods more often than others, say that. If it specializes in residential work or commercial maintenance, say that too. Clear beats clever.

### Search-friendly structure

Title tags, meta descriptions, internal links, and service schema all matter. So do clean URLs and page copy that uses plain language.

That is not fancy SEO magic. It is basic structure. Search engines and AI tools read the same signals people do, just without the coffee.

## When to rebuild instead of patching

Sometimes the problem is not one missing feature. Sometimes the whole site is the problem.

A rebuild usually makes sense when the current site is slow on phones, hard to update, buried in old plugins, or impossible to find in search. It also makes sense when the business has grown past the old site.

Look for these signs:

- The phone number is hard to find
- Forms break or go nowhere
- Service pages are thin or missing
- New photos are hard to add
- Seasonal pages are a pain to update
- The design looks outdated enough to vote against

If three or more of those are true, patching usually becomes a hobby. Rebuilding is the better move.

## Bottom line

A landscaping website in 2026 should do more than look professional. It should help the company win spring cleanup calls, hardscape projects, maintenance contracts, and seasonal jobs without making people hunt for a phone number.

For most small landscaping companies, a realistic budget is $1,500 to $4,000 for a basic site, $4,000 to $10,000 for a growth site, and $10,000 to $25,000+ for a more advanced build.

Cheap can work when the scope is tight. But if the site never brings in leads, it is just decorative mulch.
