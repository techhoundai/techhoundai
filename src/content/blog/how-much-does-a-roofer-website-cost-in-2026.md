# How Much Does a Roofer Website Cost in 2026?

A roofer website is not a digital brochure. It has to build trust fast, answer pricing questions, and push people to call before they keep shopping.

That is why roofing sites cost more than a basic five-page business site. The price depends on how much lead generation, local SEO, and content work the site has to do.

## What a roofer website actually has to do

A roofing site needs to do three jobs well. It has to make the company look legit, explain the services clearly, and get the visitor to take action.

Most homeowners are not browsing roofing sites for fun. They are dealing with a leak, storm damage, missing shingles, or an insurance headache. They want fast answers and a short path to a quote.

That means the site needs more than a logo, a homepage, and a contact page. It needs service pages, proof, and a mobile layout that makes calling easy.

A good roofing site usually includes these basics:

- Clear service pages for repair, replacement, inspections, and storm damage
- Before and after photos
- Reviews and testimonials
- A simple estimate form
- Click-to-call buttons on mobile
- Service-area pages for the towns and neighborhoods you actually serve

If those pieces are missing, the site may look fine and still underperform. Pretty does not pay the bills. Leads do.

## Roofer website cost ranges in 2026

Here is the honest range for most U.S. roofing companies.

| Build type | Typical cost | Best for |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Starter site | $2,000 to $5,000 | New roofers, solo operators, or companies that need a solid online presence fast |
| Growth site | $5,000 to $12,000 | Established roofers who want better SEO, more pages, and better lead capture |
| Advanced site | $12,000 to $30,000+ | Multi-location companies, storm response teams, and roofers who need integrations or heavier content work |

A starter site gives you the essentials. It is usually built from a template, has a clean design, and covers the main services without much custom work.

A growth site is where most roofing companies should land. It gives room for better copy, service-area pages, trust signals, and a setup that can actually rank in search.

An advanced site makes sense when the business has more moving parts. That includes financing tools, CRM integration, quote workflows, multiple crews, or a serious SEO plan.

Ongoing costs matter too. Hosting, maintenance, backups, and small updates often run $75 to $400 per month. If SEO is part of the plan, that is usually a separate monthly cost.

## What changes the price the most

The biggest price driver is scope. Every page, feature, and piece of content adds time.

### Number of pages

A five-page site costs less than a twenty-page site. That sounds obvious, but roofing companies often need more pages than they expect.

A strong roofing site usually needs separate pages for roof repair, roof replacement, roof inspection, emergency roof tarping, storm damage, hail damage, and financing. Each page needs its own copy and structure.

If you want service-area pages for Pittsburgh, Mt. Lebanon, Bethel Park, Cranberry, and Robinson, the price goes up again. Those pages are not copy and paste jobs if they are supposed to rank.

### Photos, video, and proof

This is where cheap sites get exposed. Stock photos of shiny roofs do not convince anyone.

Real project photos, crew photos, drone shots, and short video clips make the company feel real. They also take time to collect, sort, and place in the right spots.

If the company has strong before and after photos, the website should show them. That content can do more for conversions than another fancy animation nobody asked for.

### Copywriting and SEO

Good roofing copy is not fluffy marketing text. It explains what the service is, when someone needs it, and why the company is a safe choice.

SEO adds more work because the site has to be built around search intent. That means clear page titles, proper headings, internal links, local business signals, and content that matches how homeowners search.

If the site needs to compete in a crowded market, SEO becomes a real part of the budget. In Pittsburgh, that matters. There are plenty of roofers, and nobody is waiting around for a vague homepage to impress them.

### Forms, estimates, and CRM integration

A basic contact form is cheap. A smart lead form with routing, notifications, calendar booking, or CRM sync is not.

If the company uses JobNimbus, AccuLynx, HubSpot, or another system, the website may need to send leads into that stack cleanly. That takes planning and testing.

The same goes for instant estimate tools, financing applications, or storm-damage intake forms. Those features can lift conversions, but they do not come free.

## Template vs custom: which one wins?

A template site is fine for a small roofing company that needs to get online without burning cash. It is faster, cheaper, and good enough for a simple launch.

The downside is that templates tend to look like templates. They also make it harder to build a site around the exact services, service areas, and conversion paths the company needs.

A custom site wins when the roofing business is already competing for serious local search traffic. It also wins when the company wants better branding, better content, and a website that feels like a sales tool.

That does not mean every roofer needs a giant custom build. It means the budget should match the goal. A small town roofer and a storm-response company with three crews do not need the same website.

## What a roofing website should include to get calls

A good roofing website should answer the questions people are already asking. That is the whole game.

### Home page

The home page should say exactly what the company does, where it works, and how to contact it. It should also show reviews, service highlights, and a clear call to action.

### Service pages

Each major service should get its own page. Roof repair, replacement, inspections, maintenance, storm damage, and emergency work are all different searches.

### Trust signals

Roofing is high trust work. People want to see licenses, insurance, warranties, reviews, certifications, and real project photos.

### Estimate path

The contact flow should be simple. Ask for name, phone, address, and a short description. That is enough for most first-time leads.

### Service-area pages

These matter more than people want to admit. A roofer that serves a metro area needs pages for the towns and neighborhoods that bring in jobs.

For Pittsburgh, that might mean pages for the South Hills, North Hills, Monroeville, Cranberry, and nearby suburbs. The point is not to stuff city names everywhere. The point is to show up where customers actually live.

### Emergency and storm pages

If the company does storm damage or emergency tarping, it should say so clearly. Those searches are urgent, and urgent searches convert.

## When to rebuild instead of patch the old site

Sometimes the answer is not another tweak. Sometimes the site is the problem.

A rebuild makes sense when the current site is slow, hard to edit, broken on mobile, or missing the pages that matter. It also makes sense when the design is old enough to hurt trust.

If the website cannot clearly show services, reviews, or a phone number, patching it is usually a waste. At some point, a better build costs less than endless fixes.

A good rule is simple. If the site is not helping the company win jobs, it needs more than a facelift.

## Bottom line

Most roofing companies should expect to spend $5,000 to $12,000 for a website that is built to get leads, not just sit there.

A small starter site can cost less. A multi-location or SEO-heavy build can cost much more. The right budget depends on how competitive the market is and how much the website needs to do.

The cheapest site is not always the best deal. If it does not bring in calls, it is just expensive wallpaper with a contact form.
