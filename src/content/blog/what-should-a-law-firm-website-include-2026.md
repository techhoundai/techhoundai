# What Should a Law Firm Website Include in 2026?

A law firm website is not a brochure. It is a filter. The right site helps the right client call, while the wrong one scares off good leads and attracts tire kickers.

That sounds harsh, but it is true. People visit a law firm site with one question in mind: can this firm help me, and can I trust them?

## Start With the Pages That Actually Matter

A lot of law firm sites try to be fancy before they are useful. Bad move. The first job is giving visitors a clear path to the right service, the right lawyer, and the right way to contact the office.

### Practice area pages

Every major practice area should get its own page. Do not cram personal injury, family law, estate planning, and criminal defense into one giant page and hope for the best.

Each page should explain who the service is for, the problem it solves, and what the client can expect. It should also answer basic questions in plain English, because legal jargon does not win trust.

### Attorney bios and firm story

People hire people, not logos. Attorney bios should show real experience, bar admissions, education, associations, and the kinds of cases handled.

The about page should explain how the firm works, who answers the phone, and why the firm exists. A visitor should know whether they are calling a solo practice, a small local team, or a larger shop.

### Contact page and intake paths

The contact page should be easy to find from every page. It should include phone, email, office address, hours, and a simple form that does not ask for 14 fields.

Give people more than one path in. Some want to call. Some want to fill out a form. Some want to book a consultation after work in their car outside Giant Eagle, because that is how life works.

## Build Trust Before You Ask for the Call

Legal services are high-trust by nature. People are often stressed, angry, or confused when they land on the site. The design should calm them down, not make them hunt for basic facts.

### Real proof beats fancy copy

Add bar admissions, awards, speaking appearances, memberships, and media mentions where they are relevant. Do not stuff the page with badges just to fill space.

Testimonials help, but they should feel real. If case results are shown, keep the wording careful and accurate. No one wants a site that reads like a late-night infomercial with a law degree.

### Show the office and the people

Use real photos whenever possible. A clean office shot, a team photo, and a few candid images from the actual firm are better than stock photos of smiling people in rented blazers.

Clients want to know where the office is, what it looks like, and whether it feels professional. In Pittsburgh, that matters even more when someone is choosing between firms downtown, in the North Hills, or out by Cranberry.

### Make response expectations obvious

Tell visitors what happens after they submit a form. Will someone call within one business day? Is the office open evenings? Can they expect a free consultation?

That tiny bit of clarity can raise conversions fast. People hate uncertainty more than they hate marketing.

## SEO and Local Search Are Not Optional

A law firm website that looks good but cannot be found is just an expensive hobby. Search traffic matters, especially for local intent queries like divorce lawyer near me or estate planning attorney in Pittsburgh.

### Local pages need real intent

If the firm serves multiple cities or neighborhoods, build pages that are actually useful. A location page for Pittsburgh should not just swap in the city name and call it a day.

Explain service areas, office access, parking, nearby landmarks, and what kinds of clients the firm serves there. That helps both search engines and humans.

### Use structured data

Schema markup helps search engines understand the business. For law firms, the basics usually include Organization, LocalBusiness, Attorney, FAQPage, and Review where appropriate.

It will not magically put a firm at the top of Google. It does make the site easier for search engines to read, which is the point.

### Every page should have a job

Practice pages should link to relevant bios, FAQs, and contact options. Bios should link back to relevant practice areas. Blog posts should support service pages, not float around like orphaned homework.

This is how the site builds topical authority without turning into a pile of random legal essays.

## Conversion Features That Turn Traffic Into Consultations

A law firm website should do more than inform. It should make it easy for someone to take the next step without feeling stupid or rushed.

### Keep the contact tools visible

Put phone numbers in the header and footer. On mobile, make tap-to-call obvious. If the firm offers online booking, do not hide it behind three clicks.

A sticky call button on mobile can help a lot, especially for urgent practice areas. Just do not make the site feel like a used car lot with better typography.

### Use forms that respect the user

Long forms kill leads. Ask for name, contact info, the broad issue, and maybe a short free-text field. That is enough for first contact in most cases.

If a matter needs detailed intake, save that for after the lead is warm. The website is not the place to interrogate someone who already has enough problems.

### Add practice-specific calls to action

Different cases need different messaging. A family law page might push a consultation request. An estate planning page might offer a checklist or planning guide.

The call to action should match the service and the urgency. One-size-fits-all buttons are lazy, and lazy websites usually convert like garbage.

## What a Strong Law Firm Site Looks Like in Practice

A good law firm website is usually not huge. It is focused. For a solo or small firm, the core site can often work with 6 to 10 strong pages.

A solid structure might look like this:

- Home
- About
- Practice area pages for each major service
- Attorney bio pages
- Testimonials or results, if appropriate
- FAQ page
- Contact page
- Blog or resource center

That gives the firm enough depth to rank and enough clarity to convert. It also keeps the site from turning into a maze of pages nobody remembers.

### Budget where it counts

If the budget is tight, spend on the pages that sell the work first. That means practice pages, bios, contact flow, mobile performance, and local SEO basics.

Skip the expensive gimmicks. Nobody calls a lawyer because the homepage has a fancy animation of scales of justice spinning in space.

### A realistic cost range

For a small law firm, a basic but professional website often starts around $3,000 to $8,000. A more custom build with stronger content, SEO, and integrations can land in the $10,000 to $25,000 range.

The cheapest option is rarely the cheapest in the long run. If the site does not rank or convert, it becomes an expensive placeholder.

## Common Mistakes to Avoid

Most law firm sites fail in the same few ways. The good news is that these are all fixable.

### Too much law, not enough clarity

Visitors do not want a textbook. They want answers. Use short paragraphs, clear headings, and plain language.

### Hiding the phone number

This is still weirdly common. If someone has to search for the contact info, the site is failing its main job.

### Ignoring mobile

A lot of legal searches happen on phones. If the mobile version is slow, cramped, or impossible to use with one thumb, leads will bounce.

### No local signals

A firm serving Pittsburgh should say so clearly. The site should mention neighborhoods, office location, parking, and service areas where it makes sense.

## Final Take

A law firm website should answer three questions fast: what do you handle, why should I trust you, and how do I contact you?

Get those right, and the site starts working like a real intake tool instead of a digital business card. That is the difference between a page that sits there and a site that brings in work.
