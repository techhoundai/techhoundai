# 5 Things Missing From Most Contractor Websites

Most contractor websites look fine until you try to use them. Then the cracks show up. No clear services, weak photos, buried phone number, and no reason to trust the business.

The fix is usually not a full rebuild. It is adding the pieces that help customers decide fast. For plumbers, roofers, electricians, HVAC techs, painters, carpenters, and general contractors, those pieces are what turn a site into booked jobs.

**The five things missing from most contractor websites are clear service pages, proof that the business is real, a phone-first layout, local SEO basics, and a simple way to follow up on leads.** If one of those is weak, the site leaks calls like a bad coupling.

## 5 things missing from most contractor websites

Here is the short version. Most contractor websites fail because they hide the service, the proof, or the next step.

A homepage that says "quality service since 1998" is wallpaper. Customers want to know what you fix, where you work, and how fast they can reach you. That is the whole game.

### 1. Service pages that say exactly what you do

A homepage that says "quality service since 1998" is wallpaper. Customers want to know if you handle water heaters, trenchless sewer repair, panel upgrades, emergency repairs, or full bath remodels.

Each service page should include:
- what the service is
- common problems it solves
- what is included
- a few local examples
- one clear call to action

Good service pages also help with search terms. "HVAC repair in Pittsburgh" and "furnace replacement in the South Hills" are different searches. One page can support both, but only if it is specific.

### 2. Proof that the business is real

People do not trust a contractor because the logo looks nice. They trust photos, reviews, licenses, and job details. That means before-and-after photos, crew shots, truck photos, badges, and reviews from actual customers.

A contractor website should show:
- recent project photos
- review snippets
- service area or neighborhoods served
- license and insurance info
- years in business
- team or owner photo

For a roofer in Pittsburgh, a few shots from Squirrel Hill, West Mifflin, or Cranberry will beat stock photos every time. Stock photos look like somebody bought them in a bundle with fake confidence.

### 3. A phone-first layout that works on mobile

Most calls start on a phone. If the visitor has to pinch, zoom, and hunt for the phone number, the lead is already halfway gone.

The phone number should be at the top of every page. The contact button should tap to call. The form should be short. Name, phone, zip code, and job type is enough for most jobs. Long forms kill contact rates.

A good mobile setup also includes:
- sticky call or text button
- short quote form
- fast load time
- readable text without zooming
- buttons with enough spacing for thumbs

This matters even more for emergency work. Nobody dealing with a burst pipe wants a website that feels like a tax form.

### 4. Local SEO basics that actually matter

A lot of contractor websites have a logo, a homepage, and hope. Hope is not a ranking strategy.

Local SEO starts with the basics:
- correct business name, address, and phone number
- service pages for your main jobs
- city or neighborhood references where they make sense
- Google Business Profile linked to the site
- schema markup for the business type
- a fast, clean website

If a business serves the North Hills, Beaver County, and Monroeville, the site should say that in plain English. Do not stuff city names into every sentence like a robot with a grudge. One clear mention per page is usually enough.

Also, make sure the website matches the Google Business Profile. Mismatched hours, phone numbers, or service areas create confusion. Google hates confusion almost as much as customers do.

### 5. A simple way to turn visitors into leads

Traffic without lead capture is a waste of money and time. A contractor website should do more than look nice. It should give the visitor a next step.

That next step can be:
- call now
- request a quote
- text the office
- book a service call
- upload a photo of the issue

The best websites make it easy for a busy homeowner to take action in under 30 seconds. That is the real job.

For commercial work, add a separate path for property managers, builders, or facilities teams. They need different details than a homeowner with a broken furnace. One form for everybody is lazy. Lazy sites lose money.

## Why contractor websites lose jobs even when they look good

Pretty design hides bad structure. That is the trap.

A site can have nice colors, a modern font, and a hero image of a happy technician, then still fail because it does not answer the obvious questions. What do you fix? Where do you work? Can I reach you now? Why should I trust you?

Most lost leads come from one of these problems:
- the service is vague
- the phone number is hard to find
- the site loads slowly on mobile
- there is no proof of past work
- the customer has no clear next step

If the homepage tries to be clever, it usually gets in the way. Contractors do not need poetry. They need calls.

## What a good contractor website should include on every page

Every page should do three things fast: explain the service, prove the business is trustworthy, and make contact easy.

That means every page should have:
- a clear headline
- one or two short paragraphs about the service
- a photo from real work
- a call button
- a short form
- a link to the service area or contact page

Service pages should also answer basic questions. How fast can you come out? Do you offer emergency service? Do you handle residential and commercial work? What brands, materials, or systems do you work on?

A good contractor website feels like a good estimate. It is clear, direct, and does not waste time.

## What to fix first if your contractor website is already live

Do not rip everything apart on day one. Fix the parts that affect calls first.

Start here:
1. Put the phone number at the top of every page.
2. Add separate pages for your top three services.
3. Add real project photos.
4. Add reviews and service area details.
5. Shorten the contact form.
6. Connect the site to your Google Business Profile.

That order works because it fixes the biggest leaks first. A lot of contractors think they need a total redesign. Usually they need better words, better proof, and a page that works on a phone.

A plumber in Pittsburgh can get more calls from a boring but useful site than from a fancy one with no service pages. Same with electricians, HVAC companies, roofers, and painters. Customers are not grading design awards. They are trying to hire somebody before the problem gets worse.

## Final thoughts

Most contractor websites fail for the same reason a toolbox does. Half the tools are missing, and the ones that are there are in the wrong place.

Fix the basics first. Show the services, show the proof, make contact simple, and give local search engines something useful to index. That is how a website starts pulling its weight.

## FAQ

### What should every contractor website include?
Every contractor website should include clear service pages, a visible phone number, real project photos, reviews, and a short contact form. It should also say where the business works and what kind of jobs it takes.

### How many service pages does a contractor need?
Most contractors should start with three to five service pages. That is enough to cover the main jobs without turning the site into a junk drawer. Add more pages as the business grows.

### Do contractor websites need a blog?
Yes, if the blog answers real customer questions. A good blog can help with local SEO and give the site more chances to rank. A bad blog filled with fluff just burns time.

### How can a plumber website get more calls?
Make the phone number obvious, add a tap-to-call button on mobile, and create a page for each major service. Then add reviews and recent job photos. That combination usually beats a pretty homepage with no details.

### How often should an HVAC company update website photos?
At least once a month is a good target. New photos show the business is active and give customers proof that real work is happening. Fresh images also help the site feel current, not frozen in 2019.
