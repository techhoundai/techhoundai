# 7 Contractor Website Mistakes That Cost You Jobs

Most contractor websites lose jobs for boring reasons. They load slowly, hide the phone number, skip service pages, and fail to show trust. Fix those, and a decent website starts acting like a salesperson instead of a brochure.

The biggest contractor website mistakes are easy to spot once you know what to look for. Below are the ones that kill calls and quote requests, plus what to fix first.

## What contractor website mistakes cost you jobs?
The short answer: a contractor website loses jobs when a visitor cannot figure out what you do, where you work, and how to contact you in under 10 seconds.

That sounds simple, but plenty of sites fail all three. A homeowner in Pittsburgh or a property manager in Cranberry is not studying your homepage. They want a fast answer and a clear next step.

Common conversion killers:
- Slow load times
- No tap-to-call button on mobile
- One generic services page for everything
- Weak proof like reviews, licenses, and photos
- A form that asks for too much
- No local service area language

## 1. Slow pages and clunky mobile design
The first website mistake is simple: the page feels heavy and slow. Big hero images, autoplay video, and bloated sliders look nice to a designer. They annoy the person trying to book a plumber at 7:30 a.m.

Most contractor traffic is mobile. That means your site needs large text, short sections, and buttons that are easy to tap. If a thumb has to work too hard, the lead is gone.

Fix it like this:
- Compress every image
- Skip autoplay video on the homepage
- Keep the main headline short
- Put the phone number at the top of every page
- Add a sticky call button on mobile

A clean mobile site beats a fancy one every time. Nobody has ever booked a furnace replacement because the homepage had a cinematic animation.

## 2. One services page for every job
This is one of the biggest contractor website mistakes that cost you jobs. A single page that says “Services” tells Google almost nothing and tells customers even less.

A plumber should not bury drain cleaning, water heater repair, leak detection, and sewer work on one tiny page. An electrician should not force breaker panel upgrades, lighting installs, and EV charger installs into one paragraph.

Each major service deserves its own page. That page should explain the job, who needs it, common problems, and how the company handles it. Add a few real photos and a simple call to action.

Good rule:
- 3 to 5 core service pages for smaller shops
- 6 to 10 pages for established companies with more trucks on the road

Local pages help too. A roofer serving Pittsburgh, Mt. Lebanon, Dormont, and Bethel Park should say that clearly. Search engines like specific language. Customers do too.

## 3. No trust signals
People do not hire contractors based on hope. They hire based on proof. If the website looks generic, they assume the work will be generic too.

The trust signals that matter most are boring, but effective:
- Google reviews
- Before and after photos
- License and insurance info
- Team names and faces
- Years in business
- Warranty or guarantee details

A homepage with stock photos and vague copy feels fake. A homepage with real job photos from a Pittsburgh attic, boiler room, or roof repair feels real fast.

Put reviews near the top of the page. Do not hide them on a separate tab like a guilty secret. Also, use the exact words customers use. “Fixed our furnace same day” sounds better than “delivered exceptional heating solutions.” One sounds human. The other sounds like a brochure wrote itself.

## 4. Weak headlines and generic copy
A lot of contractor websites try to sound big and impressive. That usually means the homepage says nothing useful.

Bad example:
“Quality service for all your home needs.”

Better example:
“24/7 Plumbing Repairs in Pittsburgh and Nearby South Hills Towns.”

The second version tells visitors what the company does, where it works, and when it is available. That is what wins clicks and calls.

Write copy like a trade owner talks to a customer on the phone. Use plain language. Say “furnace repair,” not “residential thermal system optimization.” Nobody needs that nonsense.

Your homepage should answer:
- What do you do?
- Where do you work?
- Why should someone trust you?
- How do they get in touch?

If any of those answers are fuzzy, jobs slip away.

## 5. Forms that ask for too much
Long forms kill leads. Nobody wants to fill out 14 fields just to ask about a leaking pipe or dead AC unit.

A good form asks for the basics:
- Name
- Phone number
- Email
- Service needed
- ZIP code or address

That is enough for the first touch. Anything else can wait until the office calls back.

Short forms work better because they feel easy. Add a clear button that says “Request a quote,” “Schedule service,” or “Call now.” Do not use “Submit.” That word belongs on tax forms, not contractor websites.

If the business gets a lot of after-hours leads, missed-call text automation helps too. A lead that lands on voicemail at 6:12 p.m. should get a fast reply, not radio silence until morning.

## 6. No local SEO basics
A website can look good and still miss jobs if it never tells Google where the company works. That is a classic contractor website mistake.

Every important page should mention the service area naturally. Include city names, nearby neighborhoods, and common suburbs where the company actually works. For Pittsburgh trades, that might mean Allentown, Brookline, Squirrel Hill, Ross Township, or Monroeville, depending on the route.

Do not stuff keywords into every sentence. That reads like a robot with a headache. Use normal language and make location clear.

Local SEO basics for contractors:
- Put the city in title tags
- Add service area pages only for real markets
- Mention towns in page copy
- Keep Google Business Profile details consistent
- Link the website to review requests and maps

A site with no local signals makes Google guess. Google is not great at guessing. It usually sends the lead to the company that explained itself better.

## 7. No clear next step
Some websites inform people and still fail to convert them. The visitor knows what the company does, but not what to do next.

Every page should have one main next step. On a service page, that might be a quote request. On a homepage, that might be a phone call. On a seasonal promotion page, that might be a short form with one offer.

Good CTA placement matters:
- Top of page
- Middle of page
- Bottom of page
- In the mobile header

Do not make people hunt for contact info. If they have to scroll like they are reading a legal contract, the lead is probably gone.

A good website reduces friction. It makes the obvious action easy. Call, text, or request a quote. That is the whole game.

## A simple contractor website fix checklist
Use this as a quick audit:
- Phone number visible in the header
- Sticky call button on mobile
- 3 to 5 service pages
- Real photos from actual jobs
- Reviews on the homepage
- City and service area language
- Short quote form
- FAQ section on key pages
- No giant homepage video
- One clear CTA per page

If the site misses more than three of these, it is probably losing jobs.

## What a good contractor website looks like in 2026
A good contractor website is not flashy. It is clear, fast, and easy to use. It says what the company does, who it helps, and how to book the next job.

That is why good sites win more calls. They do less guessing. They do more explaining. Customers like that, and so does Google.

## FAQ

### What should a contractor website include?
A contractor website should include clear service pages, a phone number in the header, customer reviews, real job photos, and a short quote form. It should also say where the company works and what kinds of jobs it handles.

### How many service pages should a contractor website have?
Most smaller trade businesses should start with 3 to 5 strong service pages. Larger companies can go deeper if they have enough demand for each service. Separate pages help customers and search engines understand the business faster.

### Why isn't my contractor website getting calls?
The usual reasons are slow load times, weak mobile design, hidden contact info, and no trust signals. A site can also fail if it only has one vague services page or no local service area language.

### Should a plumber have separate pages for drain cleaning and water heater repair?
Yes. Those are different jobs, and customers often search for them separately. Separate pages give each service a better shot at ranking and make the site easier to skim.

### How often should a contractor update website content?
At least a few times a year is a good baseline. Add new project photos, update service pages, and refresh FAQs when prices or service areas change. A stale site sends a stale message.
