# How Much Does a Nonprofit Website Cost in 2026?

A nonprofit website usually costs **$3,000 to $15,000** in 2026. Small brochure sites land on the lower end. Donation tools, event calendars, accessibility work, and CRM setup push the price up.

The short version is simple. Pay for the parts that help people give, volunteer, and trust the organization. Skip the pretty nonsense that nobody uses.

## What a nonprofit website actually needs

A nonprofit site is not just an online flyer. It has to explain the mission fast, make donations easy, and help people understand where the money goes.

For most organizations, the site should include:

- A clear homepage with a plain-English mission
- About pages for leadership, history, and board members
- Program or service pages
- A donate page that works on mobile
- Volunteer and contact forms
- Event or campaign pages
- Testimonials, impact numbers, or annual reports
- Accessibility features that real people can use

If the site serves Pittsburgh communities, local cues matter too. Mention neighborhoods, service areas, and the kind of support the organization actually offers. A food pantry in Bloomfield does not need the same messaging as a regional foundation in downtown Pittsburgh.

## Real nonprofit website cost ranges

Here is the practical range most groups run into:

| Type of site | Typical cost | Best for |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Template site | $2,000 to $5,000 | Small organizations with simple needs |
| Custom small nonprofit site | $5,000 to $12,000 | Most local nonprofits |
| Full custom site with integrations | $12,000 to $30,000+ | Larger groups, campaigns, and multiple programs |

That number usually includes design, development, copy help, and basic setup. It does not always include branding, photography, or a full content rewrite. Those pieces often get billed separately because time still exists, sadly.

Monthly costs are separate. Hosting, maintenance, backups, plugin licenses, and minor updates often run **$100 to $500 per month**. Donation processors also take a cut, usually based on the platform and payment method.

## What drives the price up

### Donation and payment setup

A simple donate button is easy. A clean donation flow with recurring gifts, receipts, and donor tracking takes more work. If the organization wants branded donation pages or multiple fund designations, expect the cost to climb.

That extra work is worth it. When people are ready to donate, friction is poison. Every extra click is a chance for them to get distracted by a dog video.

### CRM and email integrations

Many nonprofits connect their site to tools like Bloomerang, Donorbox, Mailchimp, Salesforce, HubSpot, or a volunteer platform. Those integrations save staff time, but they need setup and testing.

The more systems that need to talk to each other, the more the project turns into real software work. That is the point where a cheap website quote starts looking fake.

### Content migration

A lot of nonprofit sites have old PDFs, broken pages, and ten years of event posts nobody wants to read. Moving that content takes time, even when the content is bad.

Good migration includes cleanup. Old staff bios, outdated campaign pages, and stale links should not be copied over just because they exist.

### Accessibility and compliance

Nonprofit websites should be usable for more people, not just people with perfect eyesight and too much patience. Good color contrast, keyboard navigation, readable fonts, and proper heading structure matter.

Accessibility work is not a fancy extra. It helps visitors, improves usability, and reduces the chance of building a site that quietly excludes part of the audience.

### Custom design and copywriting

Template sites are cheaper because they reuse structure. Custom sites cost more because someone has to think through the layout, write the words, and make the pages actually fit the mission.

Good copy matters a lot for nonprofits. People do not always know the cause already. The site has to explain the problem, the solution, and why support matters without sounding like a grant application ate a thesaurus.

## DIY, freelancer, or agency?

### DIY

DIY is the cheapest path. It can work for a tiny nonprofit with a simple mission and someone on staff who likes tinkering.

The tradeoff is time. The person building the site also becomes the unpaid webmaster, content editor, and occasional bug fixer. That is rarely anyone's dream role.

### Freelancer

A freelancer is a strong middle option. This works well for small and mid-sized nonprofits that need a clean site without a giant process.

The risk is consistency. One freelancer might be great at design but weak on content strategy, SEO, or ongoing support. Another might build something pretty that breaks when staff tries to update a page.

### Agency

An agency usually costs more, but it tends to be safer for organizations that need planning, copy, design, development, and support in one place.

This is often the better fit for nonprofits that run campaigns, host events, or depend on donations. If the website is central to fundraising, the project should be treated like infrastructure, not decoration.

## Ongoing costs people forget

The first quote is not the whole story. A nonprofit website has continuing costs, and some are easy to miss.

Common extras include:

- Hosting and backups
- Security updates
- Plugin or platform licenses
- Domain renewal
- Email setup
- Form spam protection
- Analytics and conversion tracking
- Photo updates and new campaign pages

There is also the cost of bad maintenance. If the donate form breaks for two weeks, that is not a technical nuisance. That is lost revenue.

A nonprofit site also needs regular content updates. Board changes, new programs, seasonal drives, and events should be updated fast. A stale site makes the organization look smaller and less active than it really is.

## How to keep costs down without building junk

Start with the pages that actually matter. A strong homepage, donate page, about page, programs page, volunteer page, and contact page will do more than a bloated site with 40 tabs.

Pick one donation platform and stick with it. Do not build three different ways for people to give money unless there is a real reason. Confusion hurts conversion.

Write the content before design starts. When the message is clear, the layout gets simpler and the project moves faster. That saves money and avoids endless revision loops.

Use real photos when possible. Stock photos of smiling strangers in matching sweaters do not build trust. A few honest images from events, volunteers, and staff go much further.

Plan for accessibility from day one. Fixing it later costs more. Building it in from the start is cheaper and cleaner.

Finally, keep launch scope tight. Launch the core site first, then add campaign pages, blog content, and deeper SEO work after the foundation is solid. That approach keeps the budget sane and the site useful.

## Bottom line

A nonprofit website in 2026 usually costs **$3,000 to $15,000**, with larger or more complex builds going higher. The real answer depends on donation setup, accessibility, integrations, and how much content needs to be created or cleaned up.

Spend money on trust, clarity, and donations. Save money by keeping the first version focused. That is the difference between a site that exists and a site that helps the organization grow.
